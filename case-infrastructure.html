Self-hosting a business stack with AI agents

74 agentic coding sessions · 26,089,016 output tokens · 2 vCPU / 1.9 GiB RAM · Rp100,000/month · zero added infrastructure spend

The question I started with was simple: can I replace an entire business stack — Google Sheets, AppSheet, scattered forms — with something self-hosted, fast, and maintainable, without adding a single rupiah of cost?

Two months later the answer is running in production. This is what it took, what broke, and what I'd do differently.

The one-line version: I replaced a spreadsheet-and-no-code stack with a self-hosted production system — built with AI coding agents, on hardware with less RAM than a modern phone, without a backup plan that involved spending money.

The three constraints that decided everything

Architecture is never a series of unconstrained good decisions. These three limits predicted almost every technical choice that followed.

1. 2 vCPU and 1.9 GiB of RAM

No burstable CPU, no SSD guarantee. The VPS cost about Rp100,000 per month — a budget machine, not a development playground. Within the first week the constraint bit: the server choked during apt upgrade. I added a swap file. By the third week, memory pressure made the box unresponsive mid-development, which led to the zram/zswap investigation and the OOM-protection drop-in now sitting next to the service unit.

This single constraint explains the rest:

  • No Docker. Container overhead was memory we did not have. systemd services only.
  • Monitoring stayed on the private network. Exposing it would add load and attack surface.
  • Automation is killed during upgrades. When memory is the constraint, keeping automation and keeping the server responsive are real trade-offs, not best practices.
  • Playwright MCP became Playwright CLI. A memory-hog process had to go.

2. A Claude Pro subscription, not an API budget

Every token came out of a flat subscription. No API billing, no bring-your-own-key. So tokens were not a variable cost to be spent — they were a hard weekly ceiling to be planned around.

This shaped the workflow more than the architecture:

  • The /compact + /resume ritual was not optional; it was survival.
  • Moving a daily report from midnight to 01:00 was about spreading consumption across the hourly reset, not about business hours.
  • Deliberate model routing (a strong model for complex work, a cheap one for lookups) was the mechanism for stretching the cap.

3. Zero additional project budget

Total spend: VPS, domain, and the AI subscription. No CI/CD service, no managed database, no monitoring SaaS, no backup vendor.

Infrastructure as code, on a 2GB budget

The stack is reproducible from a repository, not from muscle memory. The infrastructure repo holds the installer, the backup and restore scripts, a configuration drift checker, health heartbeats, and a mirror of the host's package, proxy, service, and scheduled-task configuration.

Drift detection runs on a schedule and reports through the same heartbeat channel as backup failures — so the box tells me when reality and the repo have diverged, instead of me finding out during an incident. Backups run to off-site storage on a schedule. There is a written disaster-recovery runbook, because a restore path you haven't tested is a wish, not a plan.

What the agentic part actually looked like

The AI did not replace engineering judgment. It replaced the throughput ceiling. Across 74 sessions and 17 days of cached context, 613 million cache reads and 26 million output tokens went into one system.

The hard parts were not the code. They were:

  • Deciding what to delete. I tried two no-code backends before building anything. Both were square pegs for a round hole, and recognizing that early saved weeks.
  • Surviving the memory wall. Sessions that should have taken minutes started dying with low memory (366MB free) — retiring settled workers. The fix wasn't clever prompting; it was zram, swap, and killing a process that had no business being there.
  • Knowing when the symptom and the disease diverged. The form-broken phase went on for days. The real fix was removing the dependency on the thing that kept breaking, not debugging it again.

The transferable lesson: agentic development moves the bottleneck from typing to architecture and judgment. The sessions where I lost time were never the ones where the model was slow — they were the ones where I hadn't decided what I wanted yet.

Why this matters outside a marketing role

I built this while running marketing. The reason it exists is a marketing problem: order flow and customer communication were manual, the data lived in spreadsheets, and nobody could get a reliable number without asking me. Automating it was a growth decision, and it turned into an infrastructure one.

That is the point of this page. The marketing instinct — find the bottleneck, fix it, measure the result — did not stop at the ad budget. In most companies it stops there, because the person who owns the funnel is not the person who can deploy the fix. I have been both at once for two months, and I would rather keep being both than choose.
