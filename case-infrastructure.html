Self-hosting a business stack with AI agents

2025 – present · 2 vCPU / 1.9 GiB RAM · no spend beyond a ~Rp100k/month VPS and a domain

The question I started with was simple: can I replace an entire business stack — Google Sheets, AppSheet, scattered forms — with something self-hosted, fast, and maintainable, without spending nothing beyond a ~Rp100k/month VPS and a domain?

The answer is running in production. This is what it took, what broke, and what I'd do differently.

The one-line version: I replaced a spreadsheet-and-no-code stack with a self-hosted production system — built with AI coding agents, on hardware with less RAM than a modern phone, without a backup plan that involved spending money.

The two constraints that decided everything

Architecture is never a series of unconstrained good decisions. These two limits predicted almost every technical choice that followed.

1. 2 vCPU and 1.9 GiB of RAM

No burstable CPU, no SSD guarantee. The VPS is a budget machine, not a development playground. Within the first week the constraint bit: the server choked during apt upgrade. I added a swap file. By the third week, memory pressure made the box unresponsive mid-development, which led to the zram/zswap investigation and the OOM-protection drop-in now sitting next to the service unit.

This single constraint explains the rest:

  • No containers. Container overhead was memory we did not have. systemd services only.
  • Automation is killed during upgrades. When memory is the constraint, keeping automation and keeping the server responsive are real trade-offs, not best practices.
  • Playwright MCP became Playwright CLI. A memory-hog process had to go.

2. No spend beyond a VPS and a domain

Added spend: a ~Rp100k/month VPS and a domain. No CI/CD service, no managed database, no monitoring SaaS, no backup vendor.

Infrastructure as code, on a 2GB budget

The stack is reproducible from a repository, not from muscle memory. The infrastructure repo holds the installer, the backup and restore scripts, a configuration drift checker, health heartbeats, and a mirror of the host's package, proxy, service, and scheduled-task configuration.

Drift detection runs on a schedule and reports through the same heartbeat channel as backup failures — so the box tells me when reality and the repo have diverged, instead of me finding out during an incident. Backups run on a schedule. There is a written disaster-recovery runbook, because a restore path you haven't tested is a wish, not a plan.

What the agentic part actually looked like

The AI did not replace engineering judgment. It replaced the throughput ceiling. Across 74 sessions, the whole system was built with AI coding agents.

The hard parts were not the code. They were:

  • Deciding what to delete. I tried two no-code backends before building anything. Both were square pegs for a round hole, and recognizing that early saved weeks.
  • Surviving the memory wall. Sessions that should have taken minutes started dying with low memory (366MB free) — retiring settled workers. The fix wasn't clever prompting; it was zram, swap, and killing a process that had no business being there.
  • Knowing when the symptom and the disease diverged. The form-broken phase went on for days. The real fix was removing the dependency on the thing that kept breaking, not debugging it again.

The transferable lesson: agentic development moves the bottleneck from typing to architecture and judgment. The sessions where I lost time were never the ones where the model was slow — they were the ones where I hadn't decided what I wanted yet.

Why this matters outside a marketing role

I built this while running marketing. The reason it exists is a marketing problem: order flow and customer communication for inbound web and WhatsApp leads were manual, the data lived in spreadsheets, and nobody could get a reliable number without asking me. Automating it was a growth decision, and it turned into an infrastructure one.

That is the point of this page. The marketing instinct — find the bottleneck, fix it, measure the result — did not stop at the ad budget. In most companies it stops there, because the person who owns the funnel is not the person who can deploy the fix. I have been both at once since 2025, and I would rather keep being both than choose.
